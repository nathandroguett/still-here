Chapter 5

The Grind

'06 6June 2006 · Concord Hospital3 minute read

The chemo ward at Concord

Monday, June 5th. Concord Hospital.

They took me to the chemo ward and I stopped in the doorway for a second. It was a long, narrow room with high ceilings and tall windows, and the sunlight fell across a row of soft red reclining chairs lined up along the walls. A few beds sat together down the far end, for the people too sick to sit up. Machines, tubes, and that quiet you only get in a room where everyone is fighting something. I walked in and found my chair.

A nurse came over. Quick hands, clearly done this a thousand times. She weighed me, checked my vitals, then reached for the cannula.

Mum bolted. She couldn’t watch the needle go in, and she did that every single time for the next twelve weeks.

So it was just me and Dad when it slid into my arm. His face was set hard, the way it always went when he was carrying something he wasn’t going to show. But he didn’t look away.

This was how it would work. Four cycles, three weeks each. In the first week of every cycle, I’d have treatment five days in a row, Monday to Friday. In weeks two and three, just one day each. That was the next twelve weeks of my life.

The drip started. Saline first, to flush me through, then the actual drugs. Six hours that first day. For most of it I didn’t feel a thing, just Mum chatting away beside me, filling the room the way she fills every room. I sat there thinking, this is fine. I can do this.

Then the last bag went in. Some kind of magnesium mix. A slow burn started creeping up my arm, and nothing had prepared me for it. Deep and searing, like something was melting inside my veins. I clenched my teeth. One tear came up in my eye. I told the nurse, and she diluted it and slowed it down until it was a bearable ache. But that hour dragged on like nothing I’d ever felt. The longest hour of my life, up to that point.

The rest of that first week was the same, only longer, because the last bag had to be diluted now. The burn would always arrive late in the day. The drip pole wheeled to the bathroom and back. The cannula in my arm. Mum next to me with snacks and the newspaper. Dad coming in whenever he could get away from work. I kept telling myself it was manageable.

When that first week finished, I called my ex. She answered cold. Short. Distant, in a way that made no sense after how warm she’d been the night before chemo started. I didn’t push. I figured she’d come around. I waited for a message, a call, anything.

Nothing came. Not that week. Not the next. Not once in the whole twelve weeks. Not one text to ask how I was going. She just disappeared, completely and without a word, right when I needed someone more than I ever had. That hurt in a way I didn’t know what to do with.

Week two was so much better than week one. Instead of five days straight on the drip, I only had one session, on the Thursday. Week three was the same. Just the Thursday. By then I felt almost human again, so I went out. Called some mates, met them at the local, sank a few beers. Tried to pretend nothing had changed.

I vomited in the gutter on the way home.

Mum told the nurse on the Monday. My nurse gave me a look that was more patience than judgement. “You’ve got to be smarter,” she said, hooking me up without making a big deal of it. That stung. Not what she said, but knowing I’d let her down when she’d been nothing but kind to me.

By cycle two, my body started giving in. The fatigue moved in properly. Not the kind you can sleep off. A heaviness that settled into my bones and stayed. And then my hair. One morning there were clumps of it on my pillow. Then in the shower.

I watched it swirl down the drain and felt something close to grief.

I’d already done the big moment with the mohawk and the clippers, so there was nothing special about it this time. Just the pillow. Just the drain.

I kept cracking jokes with the nurses anyway. Kept grinning through the fog. One of the younger nurses said to me once, almost in passing,

“My daughter would be lucky to have a guy like you.”

I smiled about that for days. Even on the days I didn’t feel like smiling.

End of chapter 5
