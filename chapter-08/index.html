Chapter 8

Climbing from the Ashes

'06 9Late 2006 · Concord, NSW6 minute read

Back in the water

When they let me go that August, I walked out knowing I didn’t have to come back. That was the big thing. It wasn’t a dramatic moment. No celebration. No huge wave of relief. Just knowing, deep down, that it was over. No more ward. No more red chairs. No more drips. Dad had his arm around me. Not steering me, just ready to catch me if I went down. Mum held my hand. We walked to the car and nobody said much, and that was okay.

Halfway to the car, we ran into my cousin. She’d been living overseas and had just flown back with her husband and four kids, and they’d come straight to the hospital to see me. I barely looked up. I just kept walking, head down, one foot in front of the other. I don’t remember saying anything. Maybe I nodded. Maybe I didn’t. They just stood there as we went past. I don’t think they were ready for what they saw. They’d come expecting their cousin, and what they got was someone they hardly recognised. I think the shock of what they were witnessing kept them quiet.

Recovery wasn’t what I expected. To be honest, I don’t know what I expected. I think part of me thought that once the chemo stopped, I’d just bounce straight back. That the old me was waiting on the other side of that last session, with all the same energy and noise, still sure that nothing could touch him.

It didn’t work like that.

My body didn’t cooperate for weeks. My joints ached. Stairs were a mission. If I sat up too fast the room would spin, and I’d have to wait it out with my hands on my knees like an old man. The fatigue wasn’t normal tiredness. It was a hollow feeling that sleep didn’t fix. I’d wake up already exhausted and lie there in the grey morning, trying to remember what normal felt like.

Mum and Dad hovered. They didn’t make a fuss about it. That’s not them. But they were always just there, the way parents are after they’ve had a big enough scare that they can’t stop watching. I didn’t mind. I needed the company more than I’d admit.

A few weeks later, in early September, I’d had enough of the couch. My body still didn’t feel like mine, but my head was getting restless and I needed to do something. I got both my ears pierced, because why not. Then I called my mates and we went to the local. I walked in with a beanie pulled down low, pale and hollow-eyed, not quite the bloke they remembered, and I could feel them adjusting. The little pause. The extra warmth. The way they handed me a beer a bit too carefully. I appreciated every bit of it. I drank that beer, felt it land, and thought: okay.

One night, still bald, I was chatting up a girl at the bar. I was rusty as anything, running on whatever charm I had left. Then her boyfriend came out of nowhere and grabbed me hard by the shirt, fist ready. My mates came from every direction at once. Somewhere in the scuffle someone must have told him something, because he looked at me properly, and his face changed. He let go and walked off muttering. I just stood there. And the strange thing was, I wasn’t scared. Not even a little.

After what I’d just been through, a punch to the head would have been nothing.

He walked away, and I was still standing. That was enough.

September turned into October. My hair started coming back in the middle of the month. Short and dark, and curly, which it had never been before. I’d run my hand over it in the mornings and feel a little bit of joy I didn’t expect.

The parties came back quickly. Friday nights, Saturdays, Sunday asados, the backyard full again with family and mates and music and laughing, and for the first time in months, I felt well enough to enjoy every minute of it. I’d stand out there breathing in the smoke from the asado, and Mum would catch my eye across the yard and smile. Not the careful, worried smile she’d been wearing for three months. Her real one. The one that said we’d made it.

I wasn’t smoking like I used to. I’d gone the whole of chemo without a cigarette, but as I started feeling human again, the cravings came back. I’d have one here and there. Then one Sunday my aunty caught me mid-drag and gave me a look that could have stripped paint off a wall. “You’ve fought too hard for this,” she said. She sounded angry, but it was love. I put it out, and I never touched another one.

I got a hookah instead. Shisha most nights. Not exactly healthy, I know. But after everything I’d been through, I needed something to take the edge off that wasn’t drinking myself stupid. It did the job, more or less.

Poker kept going, too. Me and Dad, and sometimes Mum, at the local pub tables three or four nights a week. Some nights I could barely grip anything, my body still aching, but I could hold cards. My head was sharp even when nothing else was. Sitting at a table with a drink, reading the people around me, winning small pots. It was the one place where the cancer felt like it was behind me. Where I was still the guy who could read a room and come out on top.

And I was getting pretty good. One night I tried my luck at the casino, and I cleaned up. Mostly luck, I know. But I won nearly every hand. When I got up over a thousand dollars, I called Dad to come and pick me up, and kept playing while I waited. By the time he arrived I handed him about twelve hundred in chips and kept a few on the table. He did a lap and came back a couple of times, and each time I pressed a few more hundred into his hand. By the time we walked out, we had about twenty-two hundred dollars between us. To put that in perspective, I was earning twelve dollars an hour making coffees at the café. That one night was worth more than a hundred and eighty hours behind the coffee machine. For me, back then, that was huge.

A few weeks later I used that money to buy into a three-hundred-dollar tournament. I came second, and walked away with three and a half thousand dollars in cash plus a three-thousand-dollar ticket to the main event.

And I played in that main event. I played really well, too. Hand after hand, table after table, I kept going while the room got smaller around me, until I’d made it to the last three tables. Then, after the break, with the final table in sight, I didn’t win a single hand for over an hour. The blinds kept creeping up, and my stack slowly shrank. I think I finished eighteenth.

The final table was just nine places away. Those nine seats split a prize pool of three hundred and fifty thousand dollars. First place took home sixty grand, and everyone else at that table got around thirty-five thousand each. Nine places.

That might sound like a small thing to be proud of. But here’s why it mattered. One day during treatment, I was standing in the kitchen with the family and someone had left a bottle of Coke on the bench. I went to open it. Just twist the lid. The simplest thing in the world. And I couldn’t. There was no strength left in my hands. I stood there, unable to open a bottle of soft drink, and had to ask someone to do it for me. It sounds small. It wasn’t. Losing control of the tiny things hits harder than the big ones. So a few months later, sitting three tables away from a three-hundred-and-fifty-thousand-dollar final table?

Not bad for a bloke who couldn’t open a Coke bottle.

By November my hair had all grown back. Dark, and curlier than before, which I didn’t mind at all. I was smiling again. Joking again. I wasn’t the same guy I’d been before all this. I was quieter, and a bit rougher around the edges. But I was getting there.

The rollercoaster was moving again. I could feel it.

That slow click of the tracks, pulling us back up. I didn’t know where it was taking me. But for the first time in months, I wanted to find out.
