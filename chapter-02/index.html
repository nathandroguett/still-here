Chapter 2

Fortress Crumbles

'06 2February 2006 · Concord, NSW5 minute read

The house in Concord

My dad didn’t talk about money. Not to us, not at the dinner table, not ever really. All I knew was that if I needed something, I could ask. He carried things quietly and kept our life running without ever letting us see the cracks. So when it all started going wrong, I had no idea. I was barely eighteen, living in my own little world, while the house I’d grown up in was slowly coming apart around me.

It started with the Navy base. A contract at Neutral Bay. HMAS Platypus, the submarine base right on the water. You’d drive past Star City Casino on the way, through a security gate, and the place was unmistakable. A huge building on the right, an open driveway down the middle, and an undercover area on the left where the submarines sat docked at the wharf. A proper base, and a huge contract. The kind that doesn’t come along twice. Big money, and the kind of security that finally lets you breathe out after decades of pushing. Dad chased it, worked for it, and won it. I can only imagine how that felt. The man who’d arrived with nothing and spent thirty years building and refusing to be beaten, finally getting the deal that set everything up. For his kids. For their kids. Done.

Then they pulled it.

A few months in. No warning and no real explanation. They replaced him with a cheaper overseas crew and moved on. Everything he’d built and delivered, gone, like it meant nothing. Years later he’d sometimes talk about it, and there was something in his voice I’d never heard before and never wanted to hear again. It wasn’t quite anger. It was deeper than that. It was the sound of a man who had trusted the world and had that trust taken from him.

But he didn’t fold. He still had his city contracts. The skyscrapers, the big commercial clients he’d worked with for years. Relationships built on decades of turning up. So he leaned on those and kept going. Kept his crews working. Kept the business alive. He genuinely believed that if you kept doing the right thing, the world would eventually do right by you.

Then one of his biggest clients stopped paying.

Not a dispute. Not a delay. They just stopped. When Dad pushed them on it, they smoothed it over. Keep sending your crews in, they told him. It’s only temporary, we’ll sort it out. And because he trusted people, because that’s who he is, he did. Month after month he carried it, paying thirty-something wages out of his own pocket while the money that was supposed to come back never arrived.

Then they declared bankruptcy. Half a year of work. Half a year of wages he’d covered himself. Gone, with no legal way to get a cent of it back. That kind of loss doesn’t just hurt. It hollows you out.

He fought back anyway. He always does. He picked up private floor jobs, smaller contracts, whatever he could find, and threw himself into them with everything he had. He kept the house. He kept us fed. He kept going. And slowly, quietly, he started to rebuild.

Mum held everything else together, like she always did. No fuss, no complaints. She kept cooking, kept the door open, kept the house feeling like home through all of it. She was so steady that I think we all took her for granted. I know I did.

Then it happened again. Another big contract. Another client with nice words and no intention of paying. Another collapse, and this one took the last of what he had left.

The bank came. They refinanced and stretched every dollar as far as it would go, but it wasn’t enough. In February 2006, he had to sell.

The house in Concord. The double-brick fortress. The asado pit he’d built with his own hands. The furniture he’d made, piece by piece. The front gate, the path, the porch that had welcomed more people than I could ever count.

I remember the day of the auction clearly. Some days just stay with you, no matter how much you’d rather forget them.

Nobody bid.

Just silence where there should have been voices. An almost empty driveway where there should have been a crowd. I wandered inside and found Mum in one of the bedrooms with her face buried in my aunty’s shoulder, holding on to her like she’d fall over without her. My aunty didn’t say anything. There was nothing to say. I stood in the doorway and didn’t go in. I just watched.

The woman who had fed everyone, who had never let a single person leave that house without feeling loved, falling apart in a back bedroom while strangers stood in her driveway putting a price on everything she had.

They took a private offer in the end. Low. Well below what the place was worth. But they’d run out of options and out of time, and sometimes getting through means accepting things you never should have had to.

We didn’t have to leave straight away, though. The buyers were investors, not a family moving in, so my parents signed a lease and stayed on as tenants. On the surface, nothing changed. Same walls, same rooms, same porch. Except it wasn’t ours anymore. After decades of work, my parents went from owning their home to renting it, just like that. I don’t think I understood what that meant at the time. I do now.

Mum was relieved that we could stay, the way you’re relieved when something awful is finally over. But relief and grief can sit right next to each other. That day, for her, they did.

Dad never showed what it did to him. Not then, and not ever really. Not to me. He’d built that place in every way that mattered, and losing it was the kind of hurt that never fully heals. You just carry it quietly and never put it down.

I was eighteen and too caught up in my own life to understand what I was watching. I didn’t know that two months later, I’d be handing my parents something else to carry. Something heavier than a house.
