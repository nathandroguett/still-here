The High Before the Drop

'06 1Early 2006 · Concord, NSW5 minute read

Sunday asado in our backyard

I had the best childhood. Filled with love, adventure and family.

By the time I was eighteen, I genuinely believed nothing could touch me.

It wasn’t arrogance, exactly. It was more of a certainty. The world was mine and it always would be. I didn’t think about consequences. I’d never needed to. Life had never once given me a reason to.

We lived in Concord, an upper-class suburb in Sydney’s Inner West. Established streets, solid houses, people who’d planted themselves there and intended to stay. Ours was one storey, four bedrooms, double brick. A front gate, a paved path, and an undercover porch at the end of it that was always open to whoever turned up. Inside, a lot of the wooden frames and furniture had been built by my dad himself. It smelled like timber and cooking. It felt permanent. That was the whole point of it.

My dad was the reason that house existed. He came to Australia from Chile with some family already here, but not much else. No real money. No safety net. Close to nothing, even if it wasn’t quite nothing. What he did have was drive, and a stubbornness that would have worn most people out before they’d even started. He found work in floor sanding, learned it, got good at it, and slowly built it into a proper business. A full cleaning and floor sanding company, with contracts in skyscrapers across the CBD and more than thirty workers on the books. He had a reputation for turning up and doing the job right. Every time I looked at that house, I was looking at thirty years of that man’s sweat.

Mum was the other half of it, and just as important. She came from Uruguay. Green eyes, warm, and steady in a way nothing seemed to shake. She fed everyone who walked through that door, and people walked through constantly. Neighbours, cousins, family friends, friends of friends. You didn’t need an invitation. You just turned up, and within an hour you were family. That was her gift. Dad built the walls, but Mum made it a place people wanted to be.

They were fun parents. The kind who said yes to almost everything, not because they were soft, but because they believed in letting us live. They were the last to leave every party and the loudest laughing at midnight, long after other families had gone home. Every gathering, every asado, every holiday. Life was there to be enjoyed, and they showed us how.

There was one rule, never said out loud but never broken: at family events, you spoke Spanish. So when I started school, my English needed some work. I picked it up fast. I always did.

By high school I’d found my people. A tight group that formed early and stuck. We got on with every other group without trying, moved between them easily, and never really had enemies. I did well with the girls, and I won’t pretend I didn’t enjoy it. Life felt easy and full, and like it was heading somewhere good.

Then there was us, the kids. My brother was older, from Mum’s first marriage, but he was my brother through and through. The technical stuff never meant a thing to me. He’d moved to Melbourne by then to build his own life, and we were proud of him for it, even if I missed having him around more than I’d say.

My sister and I were close in age, which meant we were rivals in pretty much everything. We fought a lot growing up. Neither of us ever wanted to back down. Normal brother and sister stuff. But underneath it there was love, the family kind. When it actually mattered, we had each other’s backs.

And then there were my Abuelos. Abuela was gold-hearted and genuinely happy. One of those people who made everything feel lighter just by being in the room. She laughed easily and loved without conditions, and she passed every bit of that down to my mum. You could see it in both of them. The same warmth. The same instinct to pull people in close. Mum was who she was because of her.

Abuelo had a title: the Asado King. He’d earned it over decades of fire and smoke and patience. Every Sunday morning he’d take his spot at the asado pit Dad had built at the back of the yard, and nothing was moving him from it. Beef short ribs, chorizo, mollejas, chinchulines, morcilla. He knew exactly what he was doing and had no interest in explaining it to anyone. The coals were going by ten. By midday the backyard was full. Twenty, sometimes thirty people. Someone might bring a bottle of wine or some snacks, but Mum and Dad covered everything else. The meat, the scotch, the beers. They never asked anyone for a thing. Dominoes on the table. Arguments about nothing. Cold drinks going around. And Abuelo at the fire, all day.

I grew up in the middle of all that and thought it was just how life worked.

Outside the house, I had my own world going. I was in my second year at Sydney Uni, studying Nuclear Medicine Technology, which sounds serious. For me, at eighteen, it mostly wasn’t. I worked shifts at Majors Bay Café, making coffee and waiting tables, and the money went straight to cold beers and Friday nights. I was short and lean, with dark hair spiked up with gel and a mouth that never stopped. I could talk to anyone, and I meant it. I’d walk into a room and it would get louder. I didn’t try. That was just me.

I smoked like I’d been doing it since I was fifteen, because I had. Never gave it a second thought.

Friday and Saturday nights, my mates and my sister’s mates would pack out our place. Music up, the backyard going off. Or we’d head to the local and talk rubbish over the pool tables until closing. I’d drink schooners, crack jokes, talk to anyone nearby, and do it all again the next week. No plan. No worries. Just a young bloke who had no idea what was coming.

The thing about a rollercoaster is you never see the drop coming. You’re coasting along the top, wind in your face, grinning like an idiot. And then the track disappears beneath you.

I didn’t think about what came next. I didn’t need to. The house was solid. The family was loud and warm. The world was mine.

I didn’t know it yet, but 2006 was about to show me how wrong I was.

End of chapter 1
